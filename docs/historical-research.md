# Imperium — historical notes and research

Research checked 11 October 2026. These are short design notes, not a claim that the fictional campaign happened. Ancient narratives are often partisan, late, or contradictory. The game preserves the major public events through Aurelian's death, while its personal missions, cast, appointments, dialogue, battles and votes are authored fiction.

## Notes suitable for the in-game library

**The oath.** The game's oath is original writing. Its themes—obey the emperor, remain with the army, accept death in the service of the Roman state—draw on Vegetius, *Epitoma rei militaris* II.5. Vegetius wrote after the game's period and his surviving formula includes Christian language; the game does not present that later formula as a verbatim oath of AD 250. The same original text should appear at enlistment and accession. [Vegetius, Latin text, II.5](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/vegetius2.html)

**The lead seal.** The recruit's *signaculum* is a deliberate reconstruction. The martyr account of Maximilian, set in AD 295, refers to a lead object worn at the neck. Its exact shape, purpose and relation to military identification remain debated, and the text has theological aims. The game's stamped identity pendant is not a surviving securely attested AD 250 dog tag. [University of Nottingham, *A Language of Loyalty*, discussion of Maximilian and the signaculum](https://eprints.nottingham.ac.uk/59935/1/MWUK%20A%20Language%20of%20Loyalty%20Oaths%20and%20their%20roles%20in%20Late%20Antiquity.pdf)

**A composite career.** Twelve chapter titles combine military grades, temporary commands, social rank, governorship, Senate membership and imperial power. They were not a standard ladder that every soldier could climb. The compressed promotions and convenient appointments belong to the story. A *centuria* is represented as roughly eighty men at full strength; tiny board pieces are abstractions, not literal unit strengths. In late chapters the protagonist represents a commander and staff rather than a swordsman personally defeating every enemy.

**Gallienus and command.** During the 260s senior military responsibility increasingly passed to experienced equestrians rather than traditional senatorial officers. The existence, date and scope of one sweeping decree excluding senators remain contested. The rival's loss of military command dramatizes this transition; avoid stating that a securely dated universal ban instantly ended every senator's career. [University-hosted study of the debate](https://etd.ohiolink.edu/acprod/odb_etd/ws/send_file/send?accession=osu1534607518395542&disposition=inline); [Loyola-hosted *De Imperatoribus Romanis*, Valerian and Gallienus](https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/gallval.htm)

**Dux, prefect and governor.** *Dux* meant a leader or commander and covered varied commands; it is simplified here as the highest non-imperial military appointment available to the player. It is not presented as one universal rank above every legionary prefect. *Praeses* is a governor, distinct from a military grade. The protagonist's appointment in the new Dacia and the emperor's motive for sidelining him are fictional. Acting commands are an authored adaptation, not a claim to reproduce a particular surviving commission. Aurelian's recorded designation *dux equitum* illustrates one period use. [Loyola-hosted *De Imperatoribus Romanis*, Aurelian](https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/aurelian.htm)

**Admission to the Senate.** The chapter's vote establishes political backing; the emperor then grants admission by *adlectio inter consulares*, admission at consular rank. The Senate does not independently elect the protagonist into its membership. The timing, hearing, blocs and opponent are fictional; the board's stance and Resolve rules are game abstractions. [University of Toulouse, Daremberg–Saglio entry on adlectio](https://dagr.univ-tlse2.fr/consulter/75/ADLECTIO/texte)

**The Curia, AD 274–276.** The Senate met in the Curia Julia, which Caesar began in 44 BC and Augustus dedicated in 29 BC. Augustus set up in it the statue of Victory brought from Tarentum, a winged goddess on a globe holding a palm and offering a wreath, with her altar; Constantius II removed the altar in 357, and Gratian removed it again in 382. The house of the game's period is the one restored under Domitian. It was gutted in the fire of 283 under Carinus and rebuilt by Diocletian to the same dimensions: the building in the Forum, with its niches, fragments of marble veneer and opus sectile floor, is his, and its roof, upper side walls and rear façade are modern. Its plan is about 18 by 27 metres and it is about 21 metres high. Three wide, low steps along each long side carried some three hundred senators' seats, the presiding magistrates sat at the far end, and three large windows stood over the bronze doors. The game's Curia (Chapters XI and XII) is an evocation, not a reconstruction. It is widened to hold the 8 × 8 chamber board between the steps. Its marbles, the patterns of its dado and floor, its niches and the windows of its far wall are the game's own, since Domitian's decoration is lost. Its Victory is a stylised figure after the type, not a copy of the lost statue. [Wikipedia, Curia Julia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curia_Julia); [Wikipedia, Altar of Victory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altar_of_Victory); [Platner and Ashby, *A Topographical Dictionary of Ancient Rome* (1929), Curia Iulia](https://digi.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/diglit/platner1929/0185)

**Aurelian's walls, AD 271–275.** Aurelian began a new circuit of walls for Rome in 271, and Probus (276–282) completed it, so a scene in 275 can show some stretches finished and others still rising. The curtain was brick-faced concrete with a square tower every 100 Roman feet (29.6 metres). The sources disagree on its first dimensions. Most give it as about 3.5 metres thick and 8 metres high, but Britannica gives about 4 metres thick and 7.2 metres high, and the city of Rome's tourism site about 6 metres high. Britannica describes a tufa concrete core faced with triangular bricks. The principal gates had two arched passages faced in travertine between two semicircular towers. The game's walls before the finale (Chapter XII) follow this evidence: 3.5 metres thick, a wall walk 6.6 metres up with merlons to 8.4 metres, a square tower every thirty metres, and a double gate faced in travertine between semicircular brick towers. Its scaffolds, the heights at which building stops, the gallery of windows over the gate and the towers' upper chambers are the game's own. [Wikipedia, Aurelian Walls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aurelian_Walls); [Britannica, Aurelian Wall](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Aurelian-Wall); [Turismo Roma, the Aurelian Walls](https://turismoroma.it/en/node/1131)

**The landmarks at the middle distance.** Each chapter shows three landmarks on the horizon beyond its fields (`LANDMARK_LAYOUT`). Most are types rather than particular buildings, and the forms of all of them are the game's own.
- **The Danube chapters (I–IV)** share theirs.
  - *Novae's stone gate.* Legio I Italica held Novae from about AD 70 until at least the 430s, behind stone walls built under the Flavians, with gates and interval and corner towers.
  - *A stone signal tower.* Towers in chains along the river passed signals; one at Mohovo was built in the late second century and still in use in the third.
  - *A Thracian burial mound.* Mounds were heaped in the region from the Bronze Age into late antiquity, some still in the Roman period, and about 15,000 survive in Bulgaria.
  - *The piers of an old bridge standing without its deck* (Chapter II). They recall Trajan's bridge at Drobeta, whose stone piers outlived its timber superstructure. Dio gives the removal to Hadrian; Wikipedia and Structurae give it to Aurelian about 270. The marsh after Abritus is far downstream, so the game's piers are an unnamed bridge's, not Trajan's.
  - The reed hut on piles, the harbour quay, the road station, the small temple and the burned farmstead are generic.
- **Chapter V.** Dura-Europos's citadel stood on the bluffs above the Euphrates at the city's eastern edge. Its necropolis, west of the city, held tall tower tombs and hypogea, underground chamber tombs. Tower tombs are best known at Palmyra, where about 180 can be traced, mostly of the first century AD. Others stood in the Euphrates valley, at Dura-Europos, Halabiye and Baghuz, often with plastered half-columns, along roads, at city entrances and on ridges. The game's tower tomb follows them in outline; the ruined watchtower is generic.
- **Chapter VI.** Edessa's cemeteries held family chamber tombs cut in the rock, the dead laid in recesses cut into their walls. The best documented, at Kızılkoyun on the hill below the citadel, range from the Hellenistic period to about AD 500. The game's tombs in the hillside, on the retreat west from Edessa, follow them. The caravan station and the temple court are generic.
- **Chapter VII.** Gallienus defeated Aureolus at the Adda east of Milan in 268 and besieged him in Mediolanum. The place was later known as Pons Aureoli, Aureolus's bridge, the traditional origin of the name Pontirolo. The bridge, the city's walls and the tall roadside tomb take the game's own forms.
- **Chapter VIII.** Sarmizegetusa Regia's large circular sanctuary was set out in andesite blocks, andesite posts and timber posts round a horseshoe-shaped apse. The Romans burned the Dacian capital's temples in the wars of 101–106, so a Dacian sanctuary seen in the 270s is a ruin. The fort gate and the warehouses are generic.
- **Chapter IX.** Its city is invented, as the in-game note on Aurelian's campaigns says (`src/data/campaign.js`). Palmyra's Great Colonnade was built in stages in the second and third centuries, and the tetrapylon at its crossing is dated by some to Diocletian's renovations at the end of the third century, by others to the second. So the game's tetrapylon and colonnades belong to its own city, not to Palmyra. The walled sanctuary and the gate are generic.
- **Chapter X.** The villa, the province's basilica and the aqueduct are generic for the new Dacia south of the river.
- **Chapter XI.** All three Roman monuments were standing in 275:
  - the Colosseum, begun under Vespasian between 70 and 72, dedicated by Titus in 80, with its top storey completed by Domitian in 82;
  - the Pantheon as Hadrian rebuilt it, dated by its brick stamps to about 118–128, with Agrippa's inscription repeated on its porch;
  - the Arch of Septimius Severus, dedicated in 203 for the Parthian victories of Severus and his sons, Geta's name later cut from its inscription.
- **Chapter XII.**
  - *The Mausoleum of Hadrian.* It was built from 134 or 135 and completed by Antoninus Pius in 139, a great drum on a square base, with a garden and, by some accounts, a gilded quadriga on top; the game's group on top is a stand-in for that. It became part of the walls only around 401, under Honorius. The game's outline is lower than the monument, so as not to fill the sky.
  - *Aurelian's Temple of the Sun.* It was dedicated on 25 December 274 in the Campus Agrippae and paid for from Palmyra's spoils. Its form is unknown, so the game's temple in a walled court is its own.
  - *The Milvian Bridge.* The censor Marcus Aemilius Scaurus rebuilt it in 109 BC; Wikipedia calls his bridge stone, Structurae gives it masonry piers under a timber deck again. The game shows the stone arches the bridge has today.

**The armour.** Painted shields, mail or scale, spears and the long *spatha* guide the silhouettes. The British Museum notes the spread of the spatha through the army by the third century. Oval shields are the game's visual default, not a claim that curved rectangular shields had vanished: Yale preserves a mid-third-century rectangular scutum from Dura-Europos as well as evidence of other painted shields. [British Museum, sword catalogue](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/H_1892-0901-452); [Yale, painted shield](https://news.yale.edu/2025/02/21/painted-shield-once-wielded-roman-soldiers); [Getty/Yale conservation study of painted shields](https://www.getty.edu/publications/mummyportraits/part-two/16/)

**Shield designs.** Each faction's shields come from a small pattern library (`src/showcase/shield-patterns.js`). The designs are original simplifications, and none claims a historical unit's insignia.
- Roman shields take victory iconography: wreaths, rosettes, rings and rays. They follow the spirit of the painted shields of mid-third-century Dura-Europos, whose rectangular scutum shows an eagle with a laurel wreath, winged Victories and a lion. The earlier wings and thunderbolt remain one design among them. [Yale, painted shield](https://news.yale.edu/2025/02/21/painted-shield-once-wielded-roman-soldiers); [ISAW, Edge of Empires](https://isaw.nyu.edu/exhibitions/edge-of-empires/highlights/shield)
- Germanic boards are marked out only in colours, as Tacitus has it (*scuta tantum lectissimis coloribus distinguunt*, Germania 6). They are drawn as the round plank shields with a central boss known from the third-century deposits at Illerup Ådal and Thorsberg. [Tacitus, Germania](https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/Latin/TacitusGermania.php); [Roman Iron Age weapon deposits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Iron_Age_weapon_deposits)
- Persian shields are wickerwork under raw hide. This follows Ammianus's description of the Persian infantry in 363, the nearest account found to the game's period. [Ammianus, Res Gestae 24.6.8](https://fgh.perseids.org/read/latinLit/stoa0023/stoa001/perseus-eng2/24.6.8-24.7.3)
- Palmyrene shields are round, as Palmyra's gods carry them in military dress. [Stela of Shadrafa](https://archeologie.culture.gouv.fr/palmyre/en/media/view/8632)

**Dress of the named men, the staff and the senators.** The generated bodies' prompts follow this evidence; the generated results depart from it where the [art record](art-generation.md#reviewed-before-shipping) says.
- Soldiers wear long-sleeved tunics, dark leggings or trousers and closed boots, with mail over the tunic. The fresco of the tribune Julius Terentius sacrificing (Dura-Europos, AD 239; Yale University Art Gallery) shows men in white tunics with purplish decoration, dark leggings and a cloak pinned at the right shoulder; a tunic fragment from Dura is about 92 cm long. Crispus's white tunic with purple bands follows it. [The fresco, Judaism and Rome](https://heurist.huma-num.fr/heurist/judaism_and_rome/view/38); [Royal Ontario Museum, the tunic](https://www.rom.on.ca/blogs/re-enactment-archaeology-and-ancient-rome-greece-weekend-iv-iv-tunic). These descriptions disagree on the cloaks' colours, which the game leaves to each figure's kit.
- Belts are single and often broad, fastened at the front with a ring buckle, the near-universal fastening of the third century's new panoply. Third-century soldiers' tombstones show the buckle. [Coulston, A.R.M.E.S.](https://a-r-m-e-s.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/141-156-Coulston.pdf); [Harvard Art Museums, circular belt buckle 2012.1.39](https://www.harvardartmuseums.org/collections/object/178399)
- Mail, not segmented plate: the game's art direction rules out *lorica segmentata* for AD 250–275, and the prompts ask for a shirt of small iron rings.
- Senators wear the toga. By the third century its *umbo*, the fold at the waist, was generally folded into a band across the chest, the toga *contabulata*. The game draws each bloc's trim along that band, a game convention: a senator's rank showed in the broad purple stripe of his tunic and in his shoes, and in the third century his toga reached his feet. The generated senators' togas reach the knee. [World History Encyclopedia, the Roman toga](https://www.worldhistory.org/article/48/the-roman-toga/)

**Abritus, AD 251.** Decius and his son died in the Gothic war. The game's retreat through marshland uses the ancient tradition of the disaster near Abritus. Accounts accusing Gallus of treachery are disputed and should appear, if at all, as soldiers' rumours. The survivor patrol and rescue are invented, and winning them does not reverse the Roman defeat. [Loyola-hosted *De Imperatoribus Romanis*, Decius](https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/decius.htm)

**Dura-Europos, about AD 256.** Chapter 5 uses a fictional Euphrates fortress inspired by Dura. Archaeology records Sasanian mines beneath its western wall and the town's fall and abandonment. The campaign may let the protagonist save a position or civilians, but must not claim that he saved historical Dura. Do not present the reconstructed use of toxic smoke as an uncontested fact. [Yale University Art Gallery, Dura-Europos](https://duraeuropos.artgallery.yale.edu/)

**Valerian, AD 260.** Shapur I captured the Roman emperor during the campaign around Edessa. Shapur's own inscription is triumphal royal testimony, and later Roman accounts differ about the circumstances and captivity. The game's eagle-bearing retreat is invented. Avoid lurid stories of Valerian's later humiliation as established fact. [Encyclopaedia Iranica, Shapur I, with translation and analysis of his inscription](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/shapur-i-old/)

**Withdrawal from Dacia.** Aurelian abandoned Rome's province north of the Danube and established a new Dacia to the south. The chronology is not precise enough to insist on a single evacuation day or year; the game uses the early 270s. Its crossing, negotiations, families, rival land claims and provincial crisis are fictional. The move of Roman administration should not be described as proof that every inhabitant left the old province. [Eutropius IX.15, ancient account](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/eutropius-short-history/eutropius-short-history-9/)

**Reunification and Rome's walls.** Aurelian's walls began in the early 270s; a scene in 275 can show work still shaping the capital. His eastern victories in 272–273 and defeat of the Gallic regime in 274 remain public history. The protagonist commands one fictional part of these campaigns rather than replacing Aurelian. The tactical scenario inspired by Palmyrene heavy cavalry is a playable adaptation, not a reconstruction of a surviving battle plan. [Loyola-hosted *De Imperatoribus Romanis*, Aurelian](https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/aurelian.htm)

**Immae, AD 272, and the feigned retreat.** Zosimus (*New History* 1.50) says that near Antioch Aurelian ordered his cavalry not to meet the Palmyrene armoured horse head on but to give way before them, as if beaten, until heat and the weight of their armour had worn out riders and horses, and then to turn and ride them down. Chapter IX's feigned retreat follows that account as a playable adaptation: the marker, the line waiting at it and the strung-out pursuers are game devices, and the battle is not a reconstruction of Immae. [Loyola-hosted *De Imperatoribus Romanis*, Aurelian](https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/aurelian.htm)

**The other missions of worlds and gameplay release 7.** The Gothic ladders at the Danube fort (Chapter IV), the Persian ram at the river gate and the sortie that burns it (Chapter V), the rearguard's fallback lines (Chapter VI), the crossing under the far bank's archers (Chapter VIII) and the relief convoy (Chapter X) are invented episodes in the manner of the period's warfare, not events of the sources. Dura's archaeology (above) records a Sasanian siege ramp and mines, not a ram at a river gate; the game claims no more.

**Defections and civil war.** An army could decide a succession without a pitched annihilation battle. Ancient sources describe Aemilian's men killing him as Valerian approached in 253; Florian was killed by his troops during the confrontation with Probus in 276. The latter is a historical comparison, not an event inside the game's altered succession. The finale's negotiated standoff is fictional and still demands the player hold the army together. [Loyola-hosted *De Imperatoribus Romanis*, Aemilian](https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/aemaem.htm); [Florian](https://www.roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/florian.htm)

**The departure from history.** Aurelian dies in 275 as he did historically. After that point the succession deliberately diverges: the protagonist gains the purple instead of the historical sequence of Tacitus, Florian and Probus. The fictional elder senator is not Tacitus in disguise. The ending is alternate history, not a lost imperial biography. Army acclamation and senatorial confirmation are dramatized rather than presented as a rigid constitutional election procedure.

**Fictional people and style.** The protagonist and recurring mentor, comrade, rival, patron, provincial ally and elder senator are invented. No real emperor's survival or major dated achievement changes before the divergence. The art is a modern painted-chronicle and fresco evocation, combining generated portraits and environments with code-drawn tactical terrain and figures. Uniforms, insignia, portrait ageing and the stripe-to-purple progression are simplified for recognition. Scene illustrations evoke regions rather than reconstructing excavated sites; the campaign map is explicitly schematic. Formation names, turn counts, influence points, votes and battlefield scale are gameplay abstractions.

## Editorial cautions for implementation

- Avoid naming a pathogen for the Plague of Cyprian or fixing a universal mortality figure: neither is established. Its origin and chronology are debated. Illness, uncertainty and disruption can appear without a diagnostic claim. [Sabine Huebner, University of Basel, research on the plague's disputed timeline](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-roman-archaeology/article/plague-of-cyprian-a-revised-view-of-the-origin-and-spread-of-a-3rdc-ce-pandemic/4DD870D473D3DDB1DF0328CE2C7E3280)
- An Aurelius family name is plausible after the citizenship grant of 212; it does not imply the fictional family can be traced to a specific recipient. [Berlin State Museums, papyrus evidence and naming discussion](https://berlpap.smb.museum/ein-kamel-auf-dienstreise/?lang=en)
- Keep Chapter 11 before the news of Aurelian's assassination if admission is granted by him. Begin the alternate succession only in Chapter 12.
- The battle objective in Chapter 7 must succeed locally while Gallienus still dies in 268. Warn/join/refuse can alter trust and debts, never the public event.
- Distinguish the protagonist's successful defence or retreat from the historical Roman defeats in Chapters 2, 5 and 6.
- The same rule applies to mercy in Chapter 9: attach it to a fictional city or neighbourhood, not a claim that the player reversed the documented destruction of Palmyra in 273.
- Chapter 8 and 9 chronology must overlap coherently: present early-270s Dacia as a detached assignment, followed by participation in the remaining reunification campaigns through 274. Do not require the player to be in two places on a precise day.
